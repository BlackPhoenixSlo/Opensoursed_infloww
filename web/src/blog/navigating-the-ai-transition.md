## Start with consent, in writing

A creator's face, body and voice are their livelihood. Before any AI work involving their likeness:

1. **Ask, do not assume.** Explain exactly what you want to do: edit photos, generate images, clone a voice, draft captions.
2. **Get written consent for each use.** "AI photo editing" and "AI voice notes" are different permissions.
3. **Let them say no** without any effect on the rest of the partnership.
4. **Let them withdraw consent** later, and agree what happens to AI material already made.

A creator who feels pushed into AI will leave, and they will tell other creators why.

## Put it in the contract

Most agency contracts were written before AI was practical. Update them to cover:

- **Who owns AI-generated material** made from the creator's likeness. The default should be the creator.
- **What happens at the end of the contract.** Models, voice clones and generated content trained on the creator should be deleted or handed over, not kept by the agency.
- **Where data goes.** Which AI providers you use, and whether they keep or train on uploads.
- **Approval rights.** The creator approves AI content before it is posted.

Encourage creators to have the updated contract reviewed independently. This is not legal advice; have a lawyer in your jurisdiction draft or review these clauses.

## Follow each platform's rules exactly

Every platform sets its own AI rules, and they differ. Common requirements include that AI content features the verified creator, that it is clearly labelled, and that fans are not misled about who they are messaging.

- **Read the current terms** of each platform you work on, and assign someone to recheck them regularly.
- **Write an internal AI policy** that follows the strictest platform you use.
- **Label consistently.** If a platform asks for a tag or a disclosure, use it every time.
- **Keep humans in fan conversations** wherever the platform requires it.

Do not build workflows around grey areas. Rules on AI are changing quickly, and a workflow that depends on a loophole can fail all at once.

## Be honest about the limits

AI tools move fast, but today they still have clear weak spots:

- **Short video only.** Generated video quality tends to drop as clips get longer.
- **Consistency.** Faces, settings and details can drift between generations.
- **Tone.** AI drafts often sound generic and need human editing to match a persona.
- **Boundaries.** A model does not know a creator's limits unless a person checks every output.
- **Shelf life.** Tools and techniques go stale within months. Avoid long commitments to any single one.

Plan for human review on everything that reaches a fan.

## Address the fear directly

Creators often worry that AI will replace them. Take that seriously.

- **Frame AI as help with chores:** editing, resizing, caption drafts, scheduling.
- **Be clear about what will not change:** the creator's identity, approval rights and income share.
- **Share results openly.** If an AI-assisted workflow saves the creator five hours a week, show them; if it does not, stop using it.

## Run a small pilot first

1. Pick one task, such as photo clean-up or caption drafts, and one or two creators who are enthusiastic.
2. Define success: hours saved, quality kept, no rule problems.
3. Run it for four to six weeks with a human approving every output.
4. Review with the creators, then expand, adjust or drop it.
