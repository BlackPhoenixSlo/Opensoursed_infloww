Not without a licence. OnlyFans' terms make you responsible for everything in your content, and fair use almost never covers a creator selling content with someone else's track behind it. The good news is that legal music is easy to find. Here's how copyright applies, and what to use instead.

## How copyright applies

Copyright gives the creator of a work the right to decide how it's copied, shared and performed. That works in your favour: you own the photos and videos you create, and you can act when others repost them.

It also applies to music. A song usually has at least two sets of rights: the composition (melody and lyrics) and the recording. Using either without permission can be infringement, even if:

- you bought the song,
- you only use a few seconds,
- you credit the artist,
- the music is quiet in the background,
- it's playing on a radio or speaker in the room while you record.

OnlyFans' [Terms of Service](https://onlyfans.com/terms) require you to own, or have permission for, everything you upload. That includes the audio.

## Why fair use rarely helps

In the US, fair use is a defence weighed case by case on four factors. Other countries have their own, often narrower, exceptions. For creator content, the factors usually point the same way:

1. **Purpose.** Content on a paid platform is commercial. Commercial use weighs against fair use, especially when the music isn't being commented on.
2. **Nature of the work.** Music is highly creative, which also weighs against fair use.
3. **Amount used.** Short clips aren't automatically safe, particularly if you use the recognisable hook.
4. **Market effect.** Using a track instead of licensing it competes with the licensing market.

Don't rely on fair use for background music. If you think you have a genuine case, ask a lawyer before posting.

This is general information, not legal advice. Copyright rules vary by country; if you are unsure about a specific case, ask a lawyer.

## What can happen

- The content can be removed after a takedown notice from the rights holder.
- The platform can apply its own consequences under its terms.
- Rights holders can pursue claims for damages.

The simplest protection is to use only music you have the right to use.

## Where to get music legally

**Royalty-free libraries.** Many services offer music licensed for commercial use, usually for a one-off fee or a subscription. Read the licence for each one: some exclude adult content or certain platforms, so check before you pay.

**Creative Commons music.** Some artists publish under Creative Commons licences. Check which licence applies. Some forbid commercial use (NC), some forbid changes (ND), and most require attribution (BY).

**Public domain recordings.** Old compositions may be in the public domain, but a specific recording can still be protected. Use recordings that are themselves marked public domain.

**Commission or create original music.** Hire a producer to make tracks for you, with a written agreement that grants you the rights you need. Or make simple tracks yourself with music software.

**Direct permission.** Ask an independent artist for written permission. Many smaller artists say yes, sometimes for a fee or a credit.

**No music at all.** Natural sound or voice works well for many formats, and removes the question entirely.

## Keep a rights file

Every time you use a track, record:

1. Track name and artist.
2. Where you got it.
3. Licence type and a copy or screenshot of the terms.
4. The date you obtained it, and any receipt.
5. Which posts use it.

If a question ever comes up, you can answer it in minutes.

## Protect your own work too

Copyright works both ways. Watermark your media, keep your original files as proof of ownership, and use takedown processes on sites that repost your content without permission.
