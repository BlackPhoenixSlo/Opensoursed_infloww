Should your agency add Fanvue? Maybe. Before you move a single creator, check four things: the AI rules, the payment flow, how discovery works, and whether you can run a clean test. Inflowwww does not connect to Fanvue, so this post has nothing to sell you except a checklist.

## 1. Read the AI and likeness rules yourself, then write them down

Fanvue is known for allowing AI-assisted content. That is exactly why you should read its current creator terms before you plan anything, rather than relying on a blog post (including this one). Rules on AI change, and the ones that matter most are about disclosure and likeness.

Do this before launch:

- Copy the platform's current wording on AI labelling into your internal wiki, with the date you read it.
- Decide how every AI-assisted profile will be labelled, and who checks it before a post goes live.
- Never build an AI persona from a real person's face or voice without their written consent. Treat celebrities and public figures as off-limits.
- Tell your chatters, in writing, how to answer a fan who asks "is this a real person?" The answer must be truthful.

If a rule is unclear, ask the platform's support team and keep their reply. Guessing is not a compliance process.

## 2. Map the payment flow from the fan's side

Fan platforms differ in how fans pay, what tip limits apply, and which wallets are offered. Those details decide how much friction a buyer hits, and friction decides conversion.

Run a dry check from a fan account:

- Subscribe, tip a small amount and buy one paid message. Note each step and each screen.
- Write down which payment methods appear for your target countries.
- Check the payout schedule and fees in the creator dashboard, not in marketing copy.

Then price around what you saw. If tipping is easy, design offers that invite tips. If checkout is long, keep the number of separate purchases low and favour bundles.

No demo call. The demo is a fan account and twenty minutes.

## 3. Treat internal discovery as a bonus, not a plan

Some platforms surface creators through their own feeds and suggestion tabs, which OnlyFans largely does not. That helps, but no agency should build a launch on an algorithm it cannot see.

A sensible split for a new profile:

- **Weeks 1-2:** send existing traffic from the creator's link-in-bio page, with one tracked link per source.
- **Weeks 2-4:** watch whether in-platform discovery adds subscribers on top. Report it as a separate line.
- **Rule:** if a profile only grows when you push outside traffic, plan staffing as if discovery adds nothing.

Post on a steady rhythm from day one. Feeds reward consistency far more than a single strong week.

## 4. Run a 30-day test with a stop rule

Diversifying is only useful if you can tell whether it worked. Pick one or two creators who already have an audience and a clear brand.

Set these before day one:

- **Success line:** a revenue or subscriber number at day 30 that justifies a second month.
- **Cost line:** chatter hours, editing hours and any tooling. Count your own time.
- **Stop rule:** what happens if the profile misses the success line. Write it down so nobody argues about it later.

Keep a simple weekly log: new subscribers, revenue by source, hours spent, and one note on what changed. Four rows of numbers beat a month of impressions.
