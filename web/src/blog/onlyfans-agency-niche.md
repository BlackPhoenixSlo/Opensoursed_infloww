A niche makes your agency easy to find for the creators you want and easy to trust once they arrive. The short version: pick the overlap between real demand, thin competition and something your team already does well, then test it before you rebrand.

## Why a generalist agency struggles

An agency that takes anyone has to win on price, because it cannot win on fit. A focused agency can say "we know your audience" and prove it with examples. That has three practical effects:

- Your outreach gets shorter, because you already know what the creator worries about.
- Your scripts, posting schedule and pricing playbook are reusable across the roster.
- Referrals come easier, because creators in one niche tend to know each other.

A niche is not forever. Many agencies start narrow and add a second focus once the first one runs smoothly.

## Step 1: Audit what you can actually deliver

Start with your team, not the market. List what you are good at in concrete terms:

- Languages your chatters write well in, and the time zones they cover.
- Platforms where someone on the team has grown an account before.
- Content formats you can support (short video, photo sets, livestreams).
- Communities you understand from the inside: fitness, gaming, cosplay, alternative fashion, a specific country.

If a niche needs a skill nobody on the team has, it is a hiring plan, not a niche.

## Step 2: Check that demand exists

Interest is not the same as paying fans. Look for evidence in public places:

- Search the niche on the social platforms you would promote on and note how active the top accounts are.
- Read creator communities and forums for the niche. What do creators complain about? Unanswered DMs, inconsistent posting and pricing are common gaps an agency can fill.
- Check whether fans in the niche already pay for related things: subscriptions, commissions, merch.

You want a niche that is established enough to have buyers, but not so crowded that a new agency has nothing to add.

## Step 3: Map the competition in a spreadsheet

Make a sheet with one row per agency. Five that serve the same creators directly, and two or three that serve a neighbouring audience, is a good start. Columns worth filling in:

| Column | What to note |
|---|---|
| Positioning | The one sentence on their homepage |
| Roster | Which creator types they show |
| Services | Chatting, marketing, content, all three |
| Pricing | Split or fee, if public |
| Reviews | What creators praise and what they complain about |

The gaps jump out once the sheet is full. If every agency in a niche promises "growth" and none mentions reporting, transparent weekly numbers are your angle. Every agency homepage also claims to be the best one. Voted #1 by nobody. We weren't in the poll.

## Step 4: Run a 90-day pilot

Do not rebuild your brand on a hunch. Sign two or three creators in the niche and run them for 90 days with clear targets agreed upfront: subscriber growth, renewal rate, and earnings per fan.

Keep a short weekly log of what worked and what did not. At the end you will know whether your team enjoys the work, whether the creators stay, and whether the numbers justify a full focus. Combined niches often work best here: a pilot in "fitness creators who stream" is easier to win than "fitness".

## Step 5: Write your position in one sentence

Finish with a sentence you can put on your site, in your outreach and in your contract intro:

"We run chat and promotion for [niche] creators in [languages or regions], and we send a report every Monday."

If you cannot fill in the brackets yet, you need more of steps 1 to 4.
