Being a creator can be lonely. You make the content, run the marketing, answer the messages and manage the money, often without being able to talk openly about your work with people around you. A network of other creators helps with all of it. This post is about building one.

## Why a network matters

**Emotional support.** Other creators understand the highs and lows without needing an explanation: a launch that flopped, a difficult fan, the stress of a policy change, a milestone nobody else would understand. Sharing those moments with peers makes them lighter and helps prevent burnout.

**Practical knowledge.** Someone in your network has already dealt with the question you are stuck on, whether it is a platform rule, a payout problem, or how they organise their taxes. Peers can help you avoid costly mistakes. For taxes and legal questions, use their experience as a pointer, then confirm with a qualified professional.

**Collaboration.** Relationships come first and collaborations follow. Creators who know and trust each other cross-promote and work together more easily.

**A collective voice.** Creators who know each other can share information about industry conditions and support each other in advocating for fair treatment.

## Where to find other creators online

Start with communities that exist for this purpose, and read each one's rules before posting:

- **Reddit.** Creator-advice communities are spaces for questions and advice; search Reddit for "OnlyFans advice" and check each one's rules. Many subreddits require some account history before you can post, so start by commenting helpfully.
- **X.** Many creators discuss the business side here. Follow and talk with creators in your niche, and join conversations rather than only posting your own.
- **Instagram and TikTok.** Both have stricter rules on adult content, but they can still work for connecting with peers through safe-for-work posts, behind-the-scenes content and creator trends.
- **Discord servers and Facebook groups.** Smaller, often more private spaces where people speak more openly. Ask people you trust for invitations to good ones.

Protect your privacy as you join. Use your creator identity, not your legal name, unless you have chosen otherwise, and think twice before sharing your location.

## Meeting in person

When it is safe and possible:

- Look for creator meetups and workshops in your area.
- Consider industry conventions and expos.
- Organise a small, casual meetup with creators you already know online.

Meet in public places at first, tell a friend where you are going, and trust your judgement about who you meet.

## Keep the network alive

A network needs care, like any relationship.

- **Give as much as you take.** Promote others' work, celebrate their wins, answer questions you know the answer to.
- **Be specific with encouragement.** "That series was great, the lighting in the second set especially" means more than a like.
- **Check in.** A short message to someone who has gone quiet can matter more than you think.
- **Respect boundaries.** Not everyone wants to talk business, collaborate or share numbers. Ask first.
- **Keep confidences.** What someone shares in a private group stays there.

## When you need more than peers

Peers are a great source of support, but they are not a replacement for professional help. If you feel persistently low, anxious or overwhelmed, talk to a doctor or a mental health professional, ideally one who is sex-work friendly. Many sex worker support organisations can point you to services that understand the work. If you are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line.

## For agencies

If you manage creators, you can help: introduce creators on your roster to each other when they want that, keep workloads sustainable, and make it easy to say "I need a week off". A creator who feels supported does better work and stays longer.
