Fans do not hate paying. They hate paying for something they thought they had already bought. Most PPV complaints come down to a mismatch between what the subscription page implied and what the inbox delivered, and that mismatch is fixable.

## The four complaints you will hear

Read enough fan complaints on Reddit and in your own DMs and the same four come up again and again:

1. **"I thought the subscription covered everything."** If the page says "exclusive content" and the feed is thin while the inbox is full of locks, the fan feels short-changed.
2. **"Other people get more than I do."** Paywalls split an audience into those who pay extra and those who do not. The second group can feel like second-class subscribers.
3. **"The preview oversold it."** A teaser that promises more than the unlock delivers is the fastest way to lose a buyer for good.
4. **"I am tired of deciding."** Every lock is a small purchase decision. Ten a week turns a relaxing subscription into a chore.

None of these say PPV does not work. They say badly framed PPV does not work.

## Make the promise on your page match the inbox

The fix starts in the bio, not in the DMs.

- **Say what the subscription includes.** "Daily feed posts, replies to every message, PPV for longer sets" is clear. "Everything you want" is a promise you will break.
- **If you sell PPV, say so.** Fans who subscribe knowing there will be extras complain far less than fans who discover it.
- **Keep the feed worth the fee.** A rough rule many creators use: someone who never buys a single PPV should still feel the subscription was fair value.
- **Never send a PPV of something that is already free on your feed.** Repackaged posts are the complaint that turns into a refund request or a public thread.

## Price and preview honestly

- **Match the preview to the unlock.** Show the real tone, length and setting. If the set is ten photos, say ten photos.
- **Put the length in the caption.** "4 min video" or "12 photos" removes the guessing that leads to regret.
- **Tier your prices.** A small entry offer, a mid offer and a premium offer let a fan pick a level instead of facing one big number.
- **Reward loyalty.** Long-time subscribers can get a discount or an early look. It answers the "others get more" complaint directly.
- **Space it out.** Fewer, better offers beat a lock in every message. A fan who opens one in three is a happier fan than one who ignores ten. How often to send is its own question: [should you send daily PPVs?]({{ '/blog/ppv-frequency/' | url }})

## When a no-PPV page makes sense

Some creators drop PPV entirely and earn from a higher subscription price, tips and custom requests. It tends to work when:

- the audience is large enough that subscriptions alone cover your target income,
- your content is easy to schedule as a steady feed, and
- "no PPV" is a selling point in your niche and you advertise it.

It is not one-way. Creators switch models as their audience changes: raise the price and drop PPV for a growth phase, then reintroduce a few offers later. If you change, tell your subscribers before the change, not after. Tips and customs still work on a no-PPV page; a tip goal under a post gives generous fans a way to say thanks without a lock.

## Measure the trade-off instead of guessing

You only know whether PPV is costing you subscribers if you look at both numbers together:

- **Renewal rate** before and after a change in PPV volume.
- **Revenue per fan**, split into subscription, PPV and tips.
- **Purchase rate per offer**: how many fans who received it bought it, so you can drop the formats nobody unlocks.
