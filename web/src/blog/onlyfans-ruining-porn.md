OnlyFans hasn't ended studio production, but it has given performers a second path: selling directly to their own audience. Many now combine both. Instead of an interview, here are the questions performers most often face when they add an independent channel to a studio career, with practical answers.

## Q: Is OnlyFans replacing studio work?

Not entirely. Studios still have budgets, crews, distribution and large audiences, including on free sites. What has changed is leverage. Performers with a following can now earn directly, choose their own collaborators and schedule, and decide what they want to make. Many treat studio work and their own page as complementary: studio scenes build reach, and the page turns reach into a direct relationship with fans.

## Q: Can I promote my page from my studio work?

Often yes, but check two things first.

1. **Your contracts.** Read every studio agreement for exclusivity clauses, non-compete terms, restrictions on mentioning your page, or limits on similar content. If anything is unclear, ask a lawyer who works with adult performers.
2. **Platform rules on promotion.** Each site where you promote has its own rules on links and adult-adjacent content. Follow them.

Then promote in ways you control: your own social accounts, your stage name on credits, and consistent branding across everything.

## Q: Can I post clips from my studio scenes?

Usually not. The studio normally owns the copyright in the footage, even though you're in it. Posting it on your page without a licence can breach copyright and OnlyFans' rules on content you don't own. If you want to use studio material:

- Ask the studio for written permission or a licence, and keep it.
- Otherwise, shoot your own original content. It's yours, and it's what subscribers usually want from a personal page anyway.

## Q: What changes when I shoot with other performers on my own page?

You take on the paperwork a studio used to handle.

- **Every collaborator must be verified** on OnlyFans and complete the platform's release process before content goes live.
- **Agree terms in writing**: what's being shot, where it will be posted, how revenue is shared, and what happens if someone wants content removed later.
- **Record-keeping laws** apply to producers of explicit content in some countries, including the US. You are now the producer. Get legal advice on what applies to you.
- **Consent is ongoing.** Check in on the day, and respect a change of mind.

## Q: How is the business different?

A studio pays per scene and handles production, marketing and distribution. On your own page, you earn from subscriptions, pay-per-view, messages, tips and customs, and you carry the costs and the work:

- **Production**: equipment, locations, editing.
- **Marketing**: promoting the page consistently.
- **Fan relationships**: messages and customs, often the biggest time commitment.
- **Admin**: bookkeeping, contracts, tax. Self-employment tax rules vary, and an accountant helps. This is not tax advice. Check the current rules with your country's tax authority or an accountant.

Income is less predictable but can be higher, and you keep control. Many performers find that a mix of both channels steadies the swings.

## Q: Is this good for the industry?

Many performers say direct platforms have improved their bargaining position: studios now compete for talent with an alternative that pays directly. More creators also means more diverse bodies, identities and styles reaching audiences who were poorly served before. The risks, such as leaks, harassment and pressure, still need attention from platforms and the industry.

**A checklist for the move:**

1. Read all studio contracts for exclusivity and promotion terms.
2. Don't post footage you don't own.
3. Verify every collaborator and get written agreements.
4. Learn the record-keeping obligations where you work.
5. Set up bookkeeping and tax savings from the first payout.
